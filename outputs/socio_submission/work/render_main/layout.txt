   Weight Quantization and the Reliability of Turkish Linguistic
                             Measurements in EuroLLM

Abstract

Computational research on Turkish texts requires transparent evidence about the stability of its
linguistic instruments. This study audits how weight quantization changes grammatical preferences in
EuroLLM-1.7B, treating minimal-pair performance as a bounded diagnostic rather than a validated
measure of social meaning. The local FP16 reference is compared with MLX affine 8-bit and 4-bit
configurations on 1,000 deduplicated TurBLiMP pairs across four categories. Mean token log
probability defines the primary score. FP16, Q8, and Q4 achieve accuracies of 91.2%, 91.5%, and
88.4%. The Q8 difference of +0.3 percentage points is not significant and does not establish
equivalence. Q4 loses 2.8 percentage points (Holm-adjusted p = .000469), with 42 correct-to-incorrect
and 14 incorrect-to-correct transitions. Subject agreement accounts for 18 of the 28 net lost decisions.
Summed scoring increases the Q4 deficit to 3.9 percentage points, showing sensitivity to
operationalization. Exploratory analyses do not support monotonically increasing reversals with
subword fragmentation. Measured peak MLX allocator memory is 63.8% lower for Q4. A
measurement-oriented audit links these findings to requirements for validating local text-analysis
instruments: explicit constructs, paired comparisons, disaggregated errors, label review, and task-
specific validation. The experiment establishes a model-specific accuracy–memory trade-off; it does
not establish the validity of downstream social-science coding, equitable access, or equivalence across
precision settings.

Keywords: weight quantization, Turkish, measurement validity, computational social science,
morphosyntax, reproducibility.

1. Introduction

When a language model is used to turn texts into research observations, the stability of that instrument
matters alongside its computational cost. A model may help identify linguistic patterns without
supplying valid measurements of the social concepts a researcher intends to study. Grimmer and
Stewart (2013) emphasize that automated text analysis requires extensive, problem-specific validation.
This concern motivates the present question: what evidence should researchers require before treating
a memory-reduced language model as a dependable linguistic component of a Turkish text-analysis
workflow?

The empirical contribution is an audit of grammatical preference under changes in weight precision. It
addresses one part of instrument evaluation: whether the same fixed texts receive the same linguistic
decisions, how those decisions relate to benchmark labels, and how the answer depends on scoring and
sample construction. This is relevant to the preparation of computational research instruments, but it is
not a direct evaluation of sentiment, political stance, responsibility attribution, or any other social-
science variable.

Running language models on local hardware requires sufficient memory for both the weights and the
computations performed during inference. Representing weights at lower precision reduces their
storage requirements. Whether the resulting model preserves the grammatical distinctions made by its
reference counterpart is a separate empirical question. Multilingual evaluations have shown that the
effects of quantization vary across languages and tasks and that automatic metrics can underestimate
the degradation observed by human evaluators (Marchisio et al., 2024).

In Turkish, grammatical relations such as agreement, case selection, and subordination are often
expressed through morphology. A single word can contain several morphemes, while a language
model’s tokenizer can place boundaries that do not coincide with morpheme boundaries. Sentence
length and the distribution of morphemes can therefore affect the interpretation of minimal-pair scores
(Başar & Bisazza, 2026). A decline in accuracy alone does not establish which grammatical distinction
has become harder to recognize.

We evaluate three precision configurations of EuroLLM-1.7B (Martins et al., 2024) on the same
Turkish minimal pairs from TurBLiMP (Başar et al., 2025). Three empirical questions organize the
audit. RQ1 asks how 8-bit and 4-bit quantization change benchmark accuracy and individual decisions
relative to FP16. RQ2 asks whether the conclusions are stable across grammatical categories,
sentence-scoring conventions, and sensitivity analyses for related or disputed pairs. RQ3 asks what
accuracy–memory trade-off is observed under the measured local workload. An exploratory analysis
examines associations between subword fragmentation and Q4 reversals. These questions delimit the
evidence that this experiment can contribute to evaluating a linguistic instrument.

The methodological contribution is a reporting procedure that keeps numerical stability, benchmark
agreement, and application validity separate. This distinction prevents a small aggregate accuracy
difference from being interpreted as evidence that a compressed model can replace its reference in any
Turkish research task. The discussion translates the audit into requirements for a subsequent study of
actual research coding; it does not present that subsequent study as completed.

2. Related Work

2.1. Low-precision Inference

Quantization schemes with the same nominal bit width can implement different numerical operations.
LLM.int8() retains a higher-precision computation path for outlier features while using 8-bit matrix
multiplication for most values (Dettmers et al., 2022). GPTQ uses approximate second-order
information for post-training weight quantization (Frantar et al., 2023). AWQ uses activation statistics
to identify scaling factors that protect salient weights (Lin et al., 2024). SmoothQuant redistributes
quantization difficulty between activations and weights to support 8-bit computation for both (Xiao et
al., 2023). The present comparison holds the MLX affine scheme and group size fixed and varies the
weight bit width. No further training or task-specific fine-tuning was performed.

2.2. Multilingual and Turkish Evaluations

Marchisio et al. found that quantization affected languages differently across multilingual models
(Marchisio et al., 2024). Their results motivate language-specific evaluation but do not establish a
ranking of Turkish relative to other languages. Bayram et al. examined quantization in Turkish
classification and summarization tasks alongside system efficiency (Bayram et al., 2025). Our
outcome differs from those task scores: we track whether a model changes its preference between two
sentences and whether each change agrees with the benchmark label. Differences in tasks and scoring
preclude a direct ranking across the studies.

2.3. Minimal Pairs and Morphology

BLiMP uses minimally contrasting sentences to probe language models’ sensitivity to grammatical
distinctions (Warstadt et al., 2020). TurBLiMP extends this approach to 16 Turkish linguistic
phenomena, including constructions involving flexible word order and morphological subordination
(Başar et al., 2025). Başar and Bisazza investigated how morpheme counts, subword counts, length,
and tokenizer alignment with morphological boundaries affect such evaluations (Başar & Bisazza,
2026). We treat minimal-pair accuracy as a measure of sensitivity to the selected contrasts. Our token-
to-word ratio is a sentence-level proxy for fragmentation and does not measure morpheme counts.

2.4. Validation in computational text research

Adcock and Collier (2001) distinguish the conceptual and observational levels involved in
measurement and emphasize the contextual specificity of validity claims. This provides a useful
starting point for assessing language-model outputs: the meaning of a target concept must be specified
before a score can be interpreted as its indicator. For this study, the operational target is preference for
the benchmark-designated grammatical alternative, not general communicative competence or
comprehension of a social claim.

Gilardi et al. (2023) evaluate ChatGPT on annotation tasks involving tweets and news articles,
including relevance, stance, topics, and frames. Their study illustrates an application-level evaluation
in which model labels are assessed against human annotations. The present task uses sentence
likelihoods from a smaller base model and a grammatical benchmark. Findings from either setting
cannot establish validity in the other without additional evidence. A successful linguistic audit is
therefore one source of diagnostic information, rather than a substitute for a validated coding scheme.
3. Measurement Framework

3.1. Weight Approximation and Preference Margins

Groupwise affine quantization represents weights approximately using low-bit integer codes and a
scale and offset shared within each group (MLX Contributors, n.d.). Approximation error can alter the
probabilities assigned to sentence tokens without changing the preferred sentence. Conversely, a small
shift can reverse a preference when the original scores are close. For each pair, the margin is the score
of the benchmark-preferred sentence minus the score of its alternative. A positive margin indicates
agreement with the benchmark. A change of sign indicates a reversal.

An association between reversals and small absolute baseline margins is partly a consequence of this
decision rule. A point close to zero requires a smaller displacement to cross the boundary. Such an
association can identify vulnerable decisions without identifying the representation of a particular
suffix as the cause.

3.2. Tokenization and Morphosyntax

With a fixed tokenizer, changing weight precision leaves the token sequence of a given sentence
unchanged. Matching token identities across conditions is therefore a validity check. The empirical
question is whether an existing characteristic of those sequences helps explain which decisions
change. A suffix may be represented by different subword units in different contexts. The available
records permit a test using a fragmentation proxy but do not identify morpheme boundaries or changes
in internal representations.

3.3. Levels of evidence and permissible inference

We organize the audit around three distinct relationships. First, configuration stability concerns
whether changing weight precision changes the decision for a fixed pair. Second, benchmark
agreement concerns whether that decision matches the supplied label. Third, application validity
concerns whether an output measures the construct of interest in the intended corpus. The first
relationship can be evaluated without assuming that every benchmark label is correct. The second
depends on label quality. The third requires evidence that is absent from the present grammaticality
experiment.

This distinction matters because identical errors can produce perfect stability. Conversely, a reversal
can improve agreement with the benchmark. We therefore report both directions of change and retain
the underlying continuous margins. A model that reproduces the reference is not necessarily a valid
instrument, and a model that differs from it is not necessarily worse on every item. These statements
follow from the definitions of stability and accuracy; they do not assume that FP16 provides ground-
truth language judgments.
The audit also distinguishes the measurement rule from the computational configuration. The rule
specifies how token probabilities become a sentence score and how two scores become a binary
decision. The configuration specifies the model weights, tokenizer, numerical representation, and
runtime. Holding one fixed while varying the other makes their contributions observable. When both
scoring conventions and weight precision change, their effects should not be collapsed into an
unspecified change in model quality.

A possible application is the coding of who performs an action in a news sentence. Person agreement
and argument structure may be relevant to interpreting that sentence, but grammatical preference does
not itself identify an actor or establish responsibility attribution. Connecting the diagnostic to that
application would require an explicit actor-coding definition, a representative corpus, independent
human judgments, and direct evaluation of the resulting codes. This example motivates a validation
requirement; no actor-coding outcomes are inferred from the benchmark.

5. Methods

5.1. Model Configurations

EuroLLM-1.7B is a multilingual decoder-only base model that includes Turkish (Martins et al., 2024;
UTTER Project, n.d.). The local configurations shared the same architecture and tokenizer. The model
has 24 layers, a hidden dimension of 2,048, 16 attention heads, and eight key/value heads. Q8 and Q4
used affine weight quantization with a group size of 64. No chat template, conversation history,
demonstrations, or task-specific adaptation was used.

The reference is designated FP16 on the basis of the F16 tensors in the local weight file. The BF16
field in the source configuration describes a different aspect of the model. The quantized files contain
packed U32 weights and F16 auxiliary tensors. The Q4 designation therefore refers to weight
representation and does not imply that all arithmetic was performed at four bits. Project records
associate all configurations with the same base model. An incomplete archive of the exact upstream
revision, conversion commands, and tensor inventory limits independent reconstruction.

5.2. Sentence Scoring

Sentences were tokenized without automatically adding special tokens. One BOS token supplied the
initial context. No EOS token was appended or scored. Each observed sentence token was scored
conditional on the preceding context. Logits were cast to float32 before log-softmax, and the log
probabilities of the observed tokens were averaged. This cast applied to score calculation rather than
the entire model.

5.3. Paired Inference

The statistical unit was the minimal pair. We compared each quantized configuration with FP16 using
a two-sided exact McNemar test (McNemar, 1947). Let d count correct-to-incorrect transitions and u
count incorrect-to-correct transitions. Under the null hypothesis, conditional on d + u discordant pairs,
the number of transitions in one direction follows a binomial distribution with probability 0.5. We
applied Holm adjustment (Holm, 1979) to the two primary comparisons at a significance level of .05.
No time-stamped preregistration was documented.

Individual accuracy intervals used the 95% Clopper–Pearson procedure (Clopper & Pearson, 1934).
For the paired difference, we calculated separate 97.5% Clopper–Pearson intervals for u/N and d/N.
Subtracting the upper bound for degradation from the lower bound for improvement, and vice versa
for the opposite endpoint, gives a conservative interval with at least 95% coverage under the
independent-pair sampling model. These intervals apply to individual contrasts. Holm adjustment was
applied to p values.

5.4. Dependence Among Related Pairs

Distinct pairs may share a linguistic core. Within each category, we grouped pairs whose normalized
alternatives matched after removing shared leading words from a specified list: ama, ancak, ardından,
artık, aslında, ayrıca, belki, bugün, burada, evet, fakat, hadi, hemen, işte, sadece, sonra, ve, yalnız,
yani, yine, çünkü, öncelikle, özellikle, and şimdi. The resulting 776 clusters approximate related
examples. Their membership has not been validated against the original generation templates.

We used category-stratified bootstrap resampling (Efron, 1979) of complete pairs and, separately, of
complete approximate clusters. Each procedure used 20,000 replicates for the overall accuracy
contrast, with seeds 20261028 and 20261029, respectively. Categories retained equal weights.
Dependence beyond that captured by the clustering rule may remain.

For each pair, the fragmentation proxy was the arithmetic mean of the two token-to-word ratios.

T and W denote token and word counts. Words were counted as Unicode letter or digit sequences,
preserving internal apostrophes and treating hyphens as boundaries. Standalone punctuation
contributed to the token count but not the word count. The outcome of the logistic regression was any
reversal between FP16 and Q4, regardless of direction.

The unadjusted model contained fragmentation alone. The main adjusted model also included absolute
FP16 margin, mean word count, and category, with argument structure as the reference. All predictors,
including category indicators, were standardized. We added a ridge penalty equal to λ/2 times the sum
of squared slopes to the total negative log likelihood and left the intercept unpenalized. The main
penalty was λ = 1, with 0.1 and 10 examined for sensitivity. No predictive tuning or held-out classifier
evaluation was performed.

Pair and cluster resampling each used 2,000 replicates, with seeds 20261029 and 20261030. We
refitted the scales and coefficients in each replicate and normalized category weights to their original
sizes. Odds ratios were expressed per original-sample standard deviation of fragmentation. Percentile
intervals describe uncertainty in the penalized estimates and do not correct regularization bias or
multiplicity across exploratory models. Q8 had only three reversals and was not modeled separately.

5.6. Audit procedure and interpretation rules

The audit treats the pair identifier as the join key across precision settings and verifies that the same
token sequences were scored. It retains continuous scores, binary decisions, and benchmark labels so
that a reader can separate reference agreement from label agreement. Overall accuracy is accompanied
by transition counts, category-specific results, and normalization sensitivity. The resource
measurements are attached to the exact scoring workload rather than generalized to all deployments.

Interpretation follows the available evidence. A nonsignificant superiority test is not treated as proof
of equivalence. A category-specific loss is not treated as evidence of harm to a demographic group. A
memory reduction is not treated as evidence of improved access, lower energy consumption, or lower
institutional expenditure. These restrictions define the use of the audit in research planning: it
identifies decisions requiring further validation and records the limits of the evidence rather than
certifying a model for an untested application.
6. Experiments

The experiment ran on an Apple M2 MacBook with 16 GiB of unified memory, macOS 26.4.1,
Python 3.12.15, MLX 0.32.4, and MLX-LM 0.32.2. Batch size was one sentence. We processed 20
chunks of 50 pairs and recorded scoring times after loading and warm-up. The additional analyses
used NumPy 2.5.4, SciPy 1.18.1, and Matplotlib 3.11.2.

The audit covered 3,000 pair–configuration records and 6,000 sentence records. Checks included
identifiers, categories, token counts and digests, and score consistency. The maximum absolute
discrepancy between the stored mean and the sum divided by token count was 4.24 × 10 ⁻⁷.
Recomputing the original statistics produced identical results.

Mean token log probability defined the primary analysis. Summed scores, category breakdowns, pilot
exclusion, label sensitivity, margin distributions, and fragmentation models provided secondary or
exploratory analyses. The pilot included five pairs per category drawn from the main sample. A
complete rerun in separate processes reproduced every decision. It did not add independent
observations. Background load was uncontrolled during the rerun, limiting performance comparisons.

8. Discussion

8.1. Stability of the linguistic instrument

The Q4 configuration reduced measured memory use at a cost in accuracy. Q8 remained close to the
reference on this sample. The acceptable loss for deployment depends on the intended application, and
no practical tolerance was specified before analysis. The observed trade-off consequently applies to
the reported workload.

Subject agreement contributed disproportionately to the overall decline. This pattern cannot be
attributed specifically to damaged person-suffix representations: lexical choices, template structure,
and baseline difficulty vary across categories. No token- or layer-level intervention isolated an internal
mechanism. Baseline margins help describe vulnerable examples, although they are not calibrated
confidence probabilities or a validated routing criterion.
The fragmentation results complement morphology-aware evaluations (Başar & Bisazza, 2026). A
word split into several tokens need not represent a difficult grammatical contrast. Punctuation
contributes to the numerator of the proxy, and length enters both the ratio and the adjustment model.
The token-length sensitivity interval includes the neutral value, which limits any interpretation of the
negative slope as a robust linguistic effect.

Mean and summed scores yielded different absolute accuracy levels and different sizes of the Q4
deficit. Reporting both while retaining the original primary rule makes the effect of normalization
visible. Comparisons with other minimal-pair studies should account for their scoring conventions.

8.2. Implications for computational social research

The results expose a practical problem in instrument substitution. A researcher comparing only overall
accuracy might regard the observed Q4 decline as a uniform small loss. The transition table and
category breakdown show a more selective pattern: some decisions improve, others deteriorate, and
subject agreement contributes most of the net loss. If a future coding task relies on particular
constructions, its relevant error distribution may differ from the equally weighted benchmark average.
This is a reason to validate on the intended task, not evidence that the same pattern will necessarily
occur there.

The normalization analysis also locates part of the measurement uncertainty outside the weight
representation. On the same sentences, mean and summed log probability yield different accuracy
levels and different estimates of the Q4 deficit. Neither normalization can be selected solely because it
gives a favorable comparison. A research workflow should specify its scoring rule before the main
evaluation, retain an interpretable alternative as a sensitivity check, and explain what construct the
resulting score is intended to measure. Here, both rules identify a negative Q4 contrast, while
disagreeing about its size.

Benchmark labels introduce a further source of uncertainty. The nominalization examples show why a
change toward the preferred label cannot automatically be called a linguistic improvement. Context-
dependent alternatives require judgments about intended readings. Reviewing only changed pairs is
useful for diagnosing reversals but cannot estimate the prevalence of questionable labels in the full
sample. A future validation should therefore combine a probability sample from all categories with a
separately identified diagnostic sample of reversals. Those samples answer different questions and
should not be pooled without accounting for their selection.

8.3. Resource requirements and the limits of access claims

The measured reduction in allocator memory identifies a computational opportunity under one
workload. It does not establish that a particular institution or user can adopt the model, because
adoption also depends on available equipment, task requirements, maintenance, expertise, and other
constraints not observed here. Likewise, the experiment does not estimate a monetary benefit or an
environmental saving. The appropriate conclusion is narrower: a configuration with substantially
lower measured allocator memory produced less reliable agreement with the benchmark, and the
distribution of that loss deserves scrutiny before application.

The Q8 result offers a useful comparison but not a deployment recommendation. Its accuracy is close
to FP16 in this sample and its weight file and allocator memory are smaller. An equivalence or
noninferiority claim would require an application-specific acceptable loss and a suitable design.
Choosing such a tolerance after observing the present result would change the inferential question.
Researchers can use the reported intervals to plan a future evaluation, but cannot retrospectively
convert the superiority test into a prespecified equivalence test.

8.4. A validation workflow for subsequent applications

The proposed workflow begins by defining the social-science construct and its coding unit.
Researchers then select texts from the intended setting, develop a coding manual, and obtain
independent human labels before comparing model configurations. The development material must be
separated from the final evaluation, including any prompt selection or adaptation required for a base
model. A precision comparison should keep those decisions fixed. At the final stage, researchers
should examine not only individual coding errors but also changes in the aggregate quantity they
intend to report, such as the proportion of texts assigned to an actor category.

This workflow connects the current diagnostic to application validity without assuming a numerical
transfer from one to the other. For example, a 2.8-percentage-point decline in minimal-pair accuracy
does not imply a 2.8-point bias in an estimated responsibility-attribution rate. The relationship depends
on the new labels, corpus, and coding procedure. The present contribution is the documented linguistic
audit and its interpretation rules; direct validation of a social-science coding task remains an empirical
next step.

9. Limitations

We evaluated one base model, one quantization scheme, one language, and four categories. Other
model scales, monolingual Turkish models, and calibration-based methods may behave differently. No
cross-language control permits attribution to Turkish or to agglutinative languages generally. Overlap
between test sentences and pretraining data was not investigated.

Balanced category sampling does not represent natural usage or the entire benchmark. Exact
deduplication leaves related templates, and the clustering heuristic captures only selected initial-word
variants. Exploratory AI-assisted review does not substitute for blinded judgments by independent
Turkish-language experts. Exclusion analyses address only the label issues identified.
Whole-sentence rankings do not measure inflection generation, lemmatization, or morpheme tagging.
The fragmentation proxy lacks gold morpheme boundaries. The logistic model estimates a common
linear log-odds slope without category interactions or nonlinear terms. Sparse events and
regularization limit coefficient interpretation.

The pilot overlaps the main sample, the study was not preregistered, and multiplicity was not
controlled across all exploratory analyses. A persistent public experiment archive and complete
conversion manifest have not been specified for this version. Decision reproducibility on the same
inputs is distinct from independent replication on new items. Timing comparisons were not based on
repeated full runs with randomized configuration order.

No social-science corpus was coded and no downstream research estimate was compared across
precision settings. The methodological relevance of the audit therefore rests on its role in evaluating a
linguistic component before application. It cannot establish the reliability or validity of a completed
social-science measurement instrument. The proposed application workflow is a research design
recommendation, not a second experiment or an externally validated protocol.

10. Conclusion

Across 1,000 matched Turkish minimal pairs, Q8 differed from FP16 by +0.3 pp without establishing
superiority. Q4 differed by −2.8 pp, a statistically significant decline after Holm adjustment. Its 56
reversals included changes in both directions, concentrated in low-margin items. Subject agreement
accounted for most of the net decline.

The exploratory analysis did not support an increase in decision changes with greater fragmentation.
The inverse conditional association depended on the definition of length. Alongside a 63.8% reduction
in measured peak MLX allocator memory, these findings quantify an accuracy–memory trade-off for
this model, sample, and scoring procedure. Blinded expert review, verified generation templates, and
replication on another checkpoint would test the limits of the result.

For computational research using Turkish texts, the methodological lesson is to document the
measurement procedure alongside the model configuration and to evaluate both before substitution.
The reported audit makes disagreement, benchmark uncertainty, and resource scope visible. Applying
this procedure to an actual social-science coding task is necessary before drawing conclusions about
the stability of substantive research findings.
